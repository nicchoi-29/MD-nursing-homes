Are Maryland Nursing Homes Prepared to Care for a Diverse Older Population?

By Nicole Choi

In the state of Maryland, more than 1.2 million residents speak at least 42 languages besides English inside of their homes. That’s at least 22% of the state’s total population, according to 2024 estimates.

Of those residents who speak a language other than English, those who speak Khmer, Serbo-Croatian, Vietnamese, Chinese, Spanish, Korean, Japanese, and Austronesian languages, such as Ilocano, Samoan, and Hawaiian, report the highest number of speakers with limited English proficiency, according to the 2024 One-Year American Community Survey (ACS) estimates.

Why does this matter when it comes to caring for the state’s aging population?

According to five-year 2024 ACS estimates, almost 17% of the state’s population is 65 years or older. That’s about 1.03 million people in a state of 6.2 million.

Maryland has 220 nursing homes, which in this analysis is defined as a long-term comprehensive care facility that provides 24-hour care.

The number of nursing homes isn’t evenly distributed across counties. Some counties with a relatively high percentage of residents who are 65 years or older, such as Talbot, Worcester, and Kent have only two or three nursing homes. Note that this analysis doesn't look at the number of beds available in each nursing home, an important factor that warrants further examination.

When taking a closer look at language use among this older population, a little over 17% speak another English besides English in their homes.

Within that population, almost 40% report limited English proficiency (LEP), indicating a potential need for language services, such as translation, in nursing homes.

For instance, in Montgomery County, 64.4% of Asian and Pacific Island language speakers who are also 65 years or older report LEP.

And 62.3% speakers of Spanish who are 65 years or older in the county report LEP.

However, when looking at nursing home websites, specifically under services offered, there is no information about which language and translation services are provided by the facility.

But it’s important to note that the state requires the Department of Health to provide language services when requested, so not advertising those services on their websites doesn’t mean the facilities don’t provide them.

While the absence of this information on facilities’ websites shouldn’t be interpreted as evidence that these services are unavailable, the lack of publicly available information may make it more difficult for prospective nursing home residents and their families to determine whether a facility can meet their language needs and to what extent.
